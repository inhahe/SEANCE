Terrain Synth

Home Graph Basics Wavetables

Terrain Synth is the underlying engine that all of SEANCE's built-in synths use. It treats your sound source as an N-dimensional terrain — an array of sample values arranged in 1D, 2D, 3D, or higher — and a traversal that walks through that terrain over time, reading out values to produce audio. Different combinations of terrain and traversal give you wildly different synth behaviors with the same underlying machinery.

You don't usually create a Terrain Synth node directly. Instead, the Waveform synth, sampler, and other built-in instruments are all configurations of Terrain Synth under the hood. This page explains the model so you understand what those instruments are doing internally and what knobs are available to you.

The terrain

A terrain is a multi-dimensional array of sample values. The synth can build one from any of these sources:

  • 1D waveform — a single cycle of audio, like the output of the Layered Waveform editor. The terrain is a 1D array of sample values, one cycle long.
  • 1D audio file — a recorded sample of any length. The terrain is the entire audio file as a 1D array.
  • 2D image — a grayscale image where pixel brightness becomes sample amplitude. Wide images give you a long sound; tall images give you more vertical "headroom" for traversals to wander through.
  • 3D video — a video clip. Each frame's grayscale brightness becomes a 2D amplitude surface (just like the 2D image source), and the frames stack along a third, time axis. See Importing a video below.
  • N-dimensional wavetable — multiple frames stacked into a grid (Grid mode) or scattered freely in N-D space (Scatter mode), produced by the Layered Waveform editor's wavetable feature.
  • Math expression — generated procedurally from a function like sin(x*y) or cos(x)*exp(-y), evaluated at every grid coordinate.

Whatever source you pick, the result is the same kind of N-dimensional sample array. The traversal doesn't care where it came from.

Importing a video

To turn a video clip into a 3D terrain, right-click an empty spot on the graph and choose Add Node → Terrain → From Video…. This opens the Import Video dialog.

Requirement: video decoding uses the free ffmpeg tool, which must be installed and on your system PATH. If it isn't, the dialog still opens but the Choose Video… button is greyed out with a tooltip telling you how to fix it. ffmpeg is only needed while importing — once a clip is imported, the terrain is saved inside your project, so opening the project later needs neither ffmpeg nor the original video file.

Step by step:

  1. Click Choose Video… and pick a file (MP4, MOV, MKV, AVI, WebM, …). A low-resolution preview is extracted in the background.
  2. Crop in space. Drag the rectangle over the preview frame to choose which part of the picture becomes the terrain. Drag the middle to move it, the edges or corners to resize it. The area outside the box is dimmed and a live W × H px readout shows the cropped size.
  3. Crop in time. Use the Play/Pause button and the timeline bar below the preview. Drag the playhead (or click anywhere on the bar) to scrub. The two handles on the bar set the start and end of the part you keep — the kept span is highlighted, and playback loops within it.
  4. Set the output size. Output W (px) and Output H (px) set how finely the picture is sampled. They are linked by the crop's shape: change one and the other follows to keep the same aspect ratio, so you scale the region down without distorting it. Output frames sets how many time slices are sampled across your time crop — this is the depth of the 3D terrain. A live Grid: W × H × frames readout shows the total size.
  5. Click Import. The clip is decoded at full quality and baked into the node.

A video terrain plays like any other 3D terrain — try an Orbit or Lissajous traversal to sweep through the picture-over-time volume, or a Path you draw yourself. To re-crop later, right-click the node and re-open the import dialog; it remembers your file, crop, and output settings.

Generating a terrain from a program

Instead of importing a file, you can compute a terrain with a short program. Right-click an empty spot and choose Add Node → Terrain → Terrain from Program (Generate)…. This opens the Generate Terrain dialog. It works for any number of axes (1D audio, 2D image, 3D video-like, and beyond). The terrain is computed once when you click Generate and then baked into your project (compressed), so reopening the project is instant — it never re-runs your program on load.

Step by step:

  1. Pick a language. Builtin (math expression) is a single formula and is always available. Lua (when included in your build) lets you write a function with variables, ifs and loops. Python (when Python is installed) gives you the full Python language and its math library. GLSL (compute shader, GPU) (when your graphics card supports it) lets you write a shader in the GPU's C-like shading language — handy if you already know GLSL or want the GPU's huge math vocabulary; SEANCE runs it offline on a hidden OpenGL context just to bake the terrain (nothing is drawn or played on the GPU). WASM (.wasm module) (when your build includes the WASM runtime) is the odd one out: instead of typing source, you point it at an already-compiled WebAssembly file — author the generator in C, Rust or Zig against SEANCE's grid interface and ship it as a fast, deterministic binary. They all produce the same kind of terrain — pick whichever you're most comfortable writing.
  2. Pick a mode. Per-cell runs your program once for every cell — simplest, and the only mode Builtin offers. Whole-grid runs your program once and lets it fill the whole terrain itself. In Lua or Python this can do things a per-cell program can't — blur, cellular automata, FFT, or normalising the whole result — because the program reads every cell while writing every cell. In GLSL, whole-grid is a parallel GPU pass where your shader's main() writes each cell itself. A single GPU pass can't reliably read other cells' new values, so for GLSL a Passes box appears: set it above 1 to run the shader several times, each pass reading the previous pass's finished result via prev[] — that's how you do blur, diffusion, or cellular automata on the GPU. WASM only works whole-grid — a compiled module owns its own loop, so SEANCE forces the mode to whole-grid and the per-cell option stays greyed (its tooltip explains why). The whole-grid option is greyed out when the chosen language can't do it, with a tooltip explaining how to enable it.
  3. Set the dimensions. Type the size of each axis, separated by commas. The number of values you type is the number of axes: 44100 makes a 1D sound, 512, 512 a 2D image, 64, 128, 128 a 3D "video" of 64 frames. Up to 8 axes are allowed.
  4. Write the program. Either way it must produce a value between 0 and 1 per cell (think of it as brightness — 0 is the trough, 1 is the peak). In per-cell mode the program returns that value, and each cell knows where it is: c0, c1, … give the position along each axis from 0 to 1, and x, y, z, w are the first four of those scaled to 0…2π so the same formulas you'd use in the plain math expression source work here too. In whole-grid mode (Lua/Python) you write a generate() function that fills the array. The easy way is direct N-D pixel access: setAt(r, c, value) writes the cell at integer coordinates (the value comes after the coordinates), and getAt(r, c) reads one back — reads past an edge clamp to the nearest cell, so a blur like setAt(r, c, (getAt(r-1,c) + getAt(r+1,c) + getAt(r,c-1) + getAt(r,c+1)) / 4) just works without worrying about borders. (There's also a flat-index style — set(i, value) / get(i) over for i = 0…total-1, with coord(i, axis) for a cell's position — plus coordAxis, flatten, and neighbor if you prefer flat indices, but getAt/setAt are usually all you need on the CPU.) In Python specifically, if you have numpy installed you also get a ready-made grid — a numpy array shaped exactly like your terrain — so you can skip the helpers and write plain numpy: grid[r, c] = value, slice it (grid[0, :] = ramp), or fill the whole thing at once with a vectorized expression (much faster than a Python loop). Values are clamped to 0…1 for you at the end. (If numpy isn't installed, grid is None and you use the setAt/getAt helpers above instead.) Lua and WASM don't have a true multi-dimensional array type, so there they use the flat-buffer helpers. GLSL is a little different: per-cell, your code is the body of a function that returns the value (coordinates are c[0], c[1], … plus x,y,z,w and TAU for 2π); whole-grid, your code is the body of main() and writes data[gid] directly (with coordOf(idx, axis) for positions). If you set Passes above 1, your main() runs once per pass — check uPass (the pass number, starting at 0) to seed the field on pass 0 and transform it afterwards, reading the previous pass with prevAt(idx) and finding neighbours with neighbor(idx, axis, delta) (the example shows a seed-then-blur). Two more whole-grid helpers are tailored to your terrain's exact size: flatten(c0, c1, …) turns per-axis integer coordinates back into a flat data[] index (clamped to the edges), and DIM0, DIM1, … are the size of each axis as plain constants — handy when you want to index a specific cell rather than walk neighbours. WASM doesn't have an editor at all: the Browse .wasm… button replaces the code box. Your module exports a ss_generate function and fills the grid through the host's grid calls (ss_grid_set, ss_grid_get, ss_grid_coord, and friends — the same vocabulary as Lua's whole-grid helpers); the exact interface, with a worked example, is in cpp/include/soundshop_wasm.h. In any language or mode you can also pull from the ~4000 bundled factory waveforms with waveform(id, phase) — each single-cycle waveform (the same bank the Layered Waveform editor uses) has a stable integer id shown as #N in the factory-waveform browser, and phase (0…1, wrapping) reads one cycle, returning a sample from −1 to 1. GLSL takes the id directly; Builtin, Lua, and Python also accept the name as a string (e.g. waveform("AKWF sin", c0)), and in Lua/Python a waveforms["name"] map turns a name into its id — handy to resolve once into a variable (w = waveforms["AKWF sin"]) and then call waveform(w, phase) in a hot loop. Pass a coordinate like c0 as the phase to lay one cycle across an axis; map it into the 0…1 output with waveform(id, c0)*0.5 + 0.5. Click Insert example for a starting point in whichever language and mode you've chosen.
  5. Click Generate. If the program has an error, the status line tells you immediately; otherwise the terrain is built at full resolution and the node appears.

To tweak it later, right-click the node and choose Edit Source…. You can change the program, the language, the mode, the GLSL pass count, and the axis sizes; the number of axes is fixed for an existing node (make a new one if you need a different number of dimensions).

Traversal modes

The traversal is what reads samples out of the terrain to produce audio. It maps time → coordinate, so as the synth advances through samples, the coordinate moves around the terrain and the value at that coordinate becomes the next audio sample. Several traversal modes ship built-in:

  • Linear — sweeps a single axis at constant speed. For a 1D waveform terrain this is the standard wavetable behavior: cycle through one full waveform per played note pitch. For a 1D audio file it's a sample player. For 2D, it sweeps in a straight line at an angle.
  • Orbit — circles around a center point in 2D (or higher). The radius and speed are knobs. Produces evolving pad-like sounds where the traversal periodically revisits each region of the terrain.
  • Lissajous — two-dimensional patterns formed by independent X and Y oscillators with different rates. Standard Lissajous figures (figure-8, lemniscate, etc.) drawn through the terrain. Produces complex periodic timbres.
  • Path — user-defined polyline through 2D-or-higher space. Click points in the terrain visualizer to define the path; the traversal interpolates between them at a chosen speed. Two playback modes: Loop (jumps back to the start at the end) or Bounce (reverses direction at each end, ping-ponging).

The terrain visualizer (the colored grid in the synth editor) shows you the 2D slice of the terrain you're currently working with, and overlays the traversal path on top. Drawing a path: pick the draw mode in the dropdown (Click Points or Freehand) and click/drag in the visualizer.

How playing a note interacts with all this

When MIDI sends a note-on, the synth allocates a voice and starts a traversal. The note's pitch determines how fast the traversal advances through the terrain — higher notes traverse faster (so you hear the sound "speeded up", which for a 1D waveform terrain means you cycle through it more times per second, which is the same as playing the same waveform at higher pitch). Velocity affects amplitude through the Vel Sens parameter (0 = ignore velocity, 1 = full velocity sensitivity).

Multiple voices traverse independently — playing a chord runs three traversals at three different speeds simultaneously, summing their outputs.

Position knobs (wavetable terrains)

If the terrain is a wavetable (multiple frames stacked), each axis of the wavetable becomes a Position parameter on the synth. Sweeping Position morphs through the frames, so the timbre changes as you move it. With multi-axis wavetables, each Position axis is independent — one might control brightness while another controls "bell-like vs. organ-like" if you set up your frames that way.

Position parameters can be automated, modulated by signal sources (LFOs, envelopes, XY pads), or driven by velocity-aware Trigger signals. See the Wavetables page for how to build wavetable terrains.

MIDI controller behavior

Terrain Synth voices respond to standard MIDI controller messages:

  • Pitch bend — ±2 semitones by default, applies smoothly to all sustained voices in real time
  • Mod wheel (CC#1) — drives a default 6 Hz vibrato whose depth scales with the wheel position. Disable per-synth by setting the Vibrato param to 0, freeing CC#1 to be MIDI-Learned to other parameters.
  • Sustain pedal (CC#64) — holds notes through their release stage until the pedal comes up
  • Velocity — scales per-note volume; the Vel Sens param controls how much

Audio-rate signal modulation

For wavetable terrains, you can wire audio-rate Signal cables into Position pins on the synth instead of using the slider/automation route. This lets you modulate Position at audio rate — useful for FM-style timbral effects where another oscillator's waveform drives the wavetable position sample-by-sample. Param-rate (block-rate) modulation works too via the same pin; the synth reads whatever you give it. See Pin Kinds for the difference between Param and Signal.
